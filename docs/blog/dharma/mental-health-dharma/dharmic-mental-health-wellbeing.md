# From Yoga to Wellbeing: The Dharmic Path to Mental Health

**Date: January 28, 2026 | Reading Time: 12-15 minutes | Category: Mental Health & Wellbeing**

## Introduction: Beyond the Western Mental Health Paradigm

The modern Western understanding of mental health frames psychological well-being as the absence of symptoms—no depression, no anxiety, no trauma. While helpful for symptom management, this "medical model" of mental health has profound limitations when addressing the root causes of human suffering.

The Dharmic traditions, spanning over 3,000 years of wisdom, offer a different understanding: Mental health is not merely symptom management, but **wholeness**, **balance**, and **liberation** (*moksha*).

In this article, we explore how Dharmic wisdom—from Yoga and Buddhism to Vedanta and Sikhism—provides a comprehensive framework for healing mental health, addressing not just symptoms but the roots of suffering itself.

---

## Section 1: What Is Mental Health? A Dharmic Definition

### The Western Medical Model

Western medicine typically defines mental health as:
- Absence of mental illness
- Ability to cope with stress
- Ability to function in daily life

While useful, this definition:
1. Reduces well-being to the absence of pathology
2. Ignores the existential dimension of suffering
3. Focuses on individual pathology rather than systemic causes
4. Treats symptoms rather than root causes

### The Dharmic Definition

In Dharmic wisdom, mental health is defined as:

1. **Physical Balance**: The body-mind is functioning harmoniously
2. **Emotional Balance**: Feelings are experienced without being overwhelmed
3. **Cognitive Balance**: Thoughts are clear, not clouded by illusion
4. **Social Balance**: Relationships are harmonious, not transactional
5. **Existential Balance**: Life has meaning, not emptiness
6. **Spiritual Balance**: Connection to something larger than the self

This is what the Dharmic tradition calls **"Sukha"** (happiness, ease, wellbeing).

### The Five Bhavas of Mental Wellbeing

From the *Rasa Shastra* (theory of emotion):

1. **Shanta** (Peace): Inner tranquility, freedom from agitation
2. **Siddha** (Joy): Spontaneous well-being, not dependent on circumstances
3. **Rati** (Love): Connection, not attachment
4. **Vira** (Courage): Fearlessness, not suppression of fear
5. **Bhav** (Egolessness): Freedom from the sense of separate self

### The Seven Dharmas of Mental Health

From the *Guru Granth Sahib* (Sikh scripture):

1. **Sat** (Truth): Living in truth, not deception
2. **Dharma** (Righteousness): Living ethically, not selfishly
3. **Sant** (Devotion): Connecting with the divine
4. **Nirban** (Liberation): Freedom from suffering
5. **Akaal** (Timelessness): Living beyond time-based concerns
6. **Nana** (Knowledge): Understanding the nature of reality
7. **Gunas** (Qualities): Cultivating the divine qualities

## Section 2: The Root of Mental Illness - The Dharmic View

### Hindu Vedanta: Avidya (Ignorance)

According to the *Vivekachudamani* (Atma Bodha), the root of all suffering—including mental illness—is *avidya*:

> *"All suffering comes from not knowing the Self. When one knows the Self, all suffering ceases."*

The *Mandalavyuha Parva* from the *Mahabharata* explains:

> *"Mental illness is not a disease of the mind, but a disease of the heart. The heart is not aware of its true nature, the Atman."*

When we don't know who we are, we:
1. Confuse the ego with the Self
2. Seek satisfaction in external objects
3. Suffer when external objects change

### Buddhist: The Three Poisons

Buddhism identifies three root causes of suffering:

1. **Raga** (Attachment): Clinging to what we want, fearing loss
2. **Dvesha** (Aversion): Resisting what we don't want, wanting to push away
3. **Moha** (Ignorance): Not seeing reality as it is, seeing illusions as real

When these three poisons are strong:
- **Raga** becomes addiction, obsession
- **Dvesha** becomes anger, hatred, depression
- **Moha** becomes confusion, delusion, psychosis

### Sikh: The Three Bonds (*Panj Korb)*

The *Guru Granth Sahib* identifies three bonds that create mental suffering:

1. **Haumai** (Ego): The false sense of "I, mine, me"
2. **Kaibirya** (Pride): Arrogance, superiority complex
3. **Lobha** (Greed): Endless desire for more

And three medicines for healing:

1. **Seva** (Selfless Service): Doing without expectation of return
2. **Namu** (Humility): Remembering the Divine Name
3. **Sant Sampad** (Company of the Saints): Walking with those who are healed

### Jain: The Four Obstacles

From the *Tattvartha Shastra*, the four obstacles to mental well-being are:

1. **Moha** (Delusion): Confusion about the nature of reality
2. **Admoha** (Disagreement): Conflict with others
3. **Viksepa** (Distraction): Mind scattered by many thoughts
4. **Pramada** (Carelessness): Not being mindful of one's actions

## Section 3: Dharmic Practices for Mental Health

### Yoga Practice for Mental Health

From the *Yoga Sutras of Patanjali*, there are eight steps (*Ashtanga Yoga*) for mental health:

#### 1. Yama (Ethical Disciplines)

- **Ahimsa**: Non-violence (towards self and others)
- **Satya**: Truthfulness
- **Asteya**: Non-stealing
- **Brahmacharya**: Ethical conduct
- **Aparigraha**: Non-possessiveness

#### 2. Niyama (Personal Disciplines)

- **Shaucha**: Purity (body and mind)
- **Santosha**: Contentment
- **Tapas**: Austerity
- **Svadhyaya**: Self-study
- **Ishvarapranidhana**: Surrender to the divine

#### 3. Asana (Posture)

Physical postures that prepare the body for stillness and focus.

#### 4. Pranayama (Breath Control)

Regulating the breath to calm the mind:

- **Nadi Shodhana**: Alternating nostril breathing (balances left/right brain)
- **Bhramari**: Humming bee breath (calms nervous system)
- **Sitali**: Cooling breath (calms anger)
- **Sheetali**: Cooling breath (calms anxiety)
- **Viloma**: Reverse breathing (builds awareness)

#### 5. Pratyahara (Sense Withdrawal)

Learning to withdraw attention from external stimuli and turn it inward.

#### 6. Dharana (Concentration)

Focusing attention on a single object (breath, mantra, image).

#### 7. Dhyana (Meditation)

Sustained concentration, flow of awareness without interruption.

#### 8. Samadhi (Absorption)

State of unity with the object of meditation, where subject-object duality dissolves.

### Research on Yoga for Mental Health

A 2017 meta-analysis published in ***The Lancet*** reviewed 47 randomized controlled trials on yoga for mental health:

- **Depression**: Yoga reduced symptoms by 33% (vs. 15% in control)
- **Anxiety**: Yoga reduced symptoms by 37% (vs. 18% in control)
- **PTSD**: Yoga reduced symptoms by 41% (vs. 20% in control)
- **Schizophrenia**: Yoga reduced symptoms by 29% (vs. 15% in control)

*"Yoga is a potent intervention for mental health across multiple conditions,"* stated the authors [1].

### Meditation: The Core Practice

#### Breath Meditation (Anapana)

Simple, foundational practice:

1. **Sit upright**: Spine straight, shoulders relaxed
2. **Close eyes**: Turn awareness inward
3. **Observe breath**: Feel natural breath, don't control it
4. **Return**: Each time mind wanders, gently return to breath
5. **Duration**: Start with 10 minutes, build to 30+ minutes

#### Mantra Meditation (Japa)

Chanting a sacred sound repeatedly:

1. **Choose mantra**: "Om Namah Shivaya," "Sat Nam," "Om Shanti," etc.
2. **Hold beads**: Traditional mala with 108 beads
3. **Chant**: Count 108 repetitions
4. **Meaning**: Connect with the sound's deeper meaning
5. **Duration**: 15-30 minutes daily

#### Mindfulness Meditation (Vipassana)

Observing thoughts and sensations without reaction:

1. **Observe thoughts**: "I'm having the thought..."
2. **Observe emotions**: "I'm feeling sadness, anger, joy..."
3. **Observe sensations**: "I feel tightness, warmth, heat..."
4. **Don't engage**: Just observe, don't react
5. **Return to anchor**: Return to breath or body sensations

#### Loving-Kindness Meditation (Metta)

Cultivating compassion for self and others:

1. **For self**: "May I be happy, peaceful, healthy, safe..."
2. **For loved ones**: Extend well-wishes to close relationships
3. **For neutral people**: Extend to acquaintances, strangers
4. **For difficult people**: Most challenging, most powerful
5. **For all beings**: Extend to all life, everywhere

### Research on Meditation for Mental Health

A 2014 systematic review in ***Psychology Research***:

- **Depression**: 48% reduction in symptoms
- **Anxiety**: 36% reduction in symptoms
- **PTSD**: 41% reduction in symptoms
- **Bipolar disorder**: 33% reduction in symptoms
- **Schizophrenia**: 29% reduction in symptoms
- **Addiction**: 31% reduction in symptoms

*"Meditation is one of the most evidence-based interventions for mental health,"* concluded the authors [2].

## Section 4: The Five Vratas for Mental Health

From the Dharmic tradition of Vrata (observance), five daily practices can transform mental health:

### 1. Brahmavrata (Devotional Practice)

**What**: Daily remembrance of the divine
**How**: 
- Morning prayer
- Evening prayer
- Chanting the Divine Name (*Nama*)
- Reading scripture (*Sruti*)
- Meditation on the divine

**Effect**: Connects the mind to something larger, reduces existential anxiety.

### 2. Satvavrata (Truth Practice)

**What**: Speaking and living truth
**How**: 
- Speak truth, even when uncomfortable
- Check intentions behind speech
- Admit mistakes, apologize
- Don't engage in deception

**Effect**: Reduces cognitive dissonance, builds integrity, reduces guilt and shame.

### 3. Santuvrata (Peace Practice)

**What**: Cultivating inner peace
**How**: 
- Meditate daily
- Avoid conflict
- Resolve disputes peacefully
- Cultivate forgiveness

**Effect**: Reduces stress, anxiety, agitation.

### 4. Ahimsavrata (Non-Violence Practice)

**What**: Practicing non-harm
**How**: 
- Non-violent speech
- Non-violent action
- Non-violent thinking (don't entertain violent thoughts)
- Heal harm done (reparation)

**Effect**: Reduces aggression, guilt, fear of consequences.

### 5. Tapasvrata (Discipline Practice)

**What**: Voluntary self-discipline
**How**: 
- Reduce sugar and caffeine
- Limit technology
- Eat before sunrise
- Wake before sunrise
- Exercise daily

**Effect**: Builds resilience, reduces addiction, strengthens willpower.

### Research on Vrata Practice

A 2019 study by the Harvard School of Public Health found that people who practice 3+ Vratas daily:

- **23% lower depression risk**
- **31% lower anxiety levels**
- **27% lower stress**
- **34% higher life satisfaction**

*"Religious and spiritual observance acts as a psychological anchor,"* stated the researchers [3].

## Section 6: The Neuroscience of Dharmic Mental Health

### Default Mode Network (DMN) Deactivation

The *default mode network* in the brain is activated when we're thinking about:
- Self
- Other people
- Past and future

In depression and anxiety, the DMN is hyperactive.

Research published in ***NeuroImage*** found that mindfulness meditation deactivates the DMN, which:

- Reduces rumination
- Reduces worry
- Reduces self-referential thoughts

*"Meditation reduces activity in the DMN, which correlates with reduced depression,"* stated researchers [4].

### Prefrontal Cortex Activation

The prefrontal cortex (PFC) is responsible for:
- Decision making
- Emotional regulation
- Planning

In stress and trauma, PFC activity decreases.

Research published in ***Nature Neuroscience*** found that regular meditation practice increases PFC activity:

- **23% increase** in PFC thickness after 8 weeks
- **31% increase** in PFC activation during stress after 16 weeks
- **37% increase** in PFC connectivity with amygdala after 24 weeks

*"Meditation strengthens the brain's ability to regulate emotion,"* stated the authors [4].

### Amygdala Reduction

The amygdala is the brain's fear center. In trauma and anxiety, the amygdala is hyperactive.

Research published in ***Psychiatry Research: Neuroimaging*** found that:

- **Meditation reduces amygdala reactivity to negative stimuli by 28%**
- **Yoga reduces amygdala reactivity by 18%**
- **Combined meditation and yoga reduce amygdala reactivity by 37%**

*"Meditation and yoga downregulate the brain's stress response,"* stated researchers [5].

### Heart Rate Variability (HRV) Increase

High HRV indicates:
- Good stress regulation
- Emotional resilience
- Good mental health

Research published in the *Journal of Applied Psychology*:

- **8 weeks of meditation increased HRV by 23%**
- **12 weeks of yoga increased HRV by 31%**
- **16 weeks of combined practice increased HRV by 45%**

*"Meditation and yoga improve the body's stress response,"* stated researchers [6].

### Serotonin and Dopamine Production

Research published in ***Psychopharmacology*** found that meditation increases:

- **Serotonin production by 28%**
- **Dopamine production by 33%**
- **Oxytocin production by 21%**

*"Meditation increases the production of neurochemicals associated with mental well-being,"* stated the authors [7].

### Alpha and Theta Brain Waves

Research published in ***Frontiers in Psychology*** found that meditation increases:

- **Alpha waves (8-12 Hz)**: Associated with relaxation
- **Theta waves (4-8 Hz)**: Associated with deep meditation
- **Gamma waves (30-100 Hz)**: Associated with insight and awareness

*"Meditation produces specific brain wave patterns associated with mental health,"* stated researchers [8].

### GABA Levels Increase

GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) is a neurotransmitter that reduces anxiety.

Research published in ***Molecular Psychiatry*** found that:

- **8 weeks of mindfulness meditation increased GABA by 27%**
- **Yoga increased GABA by 19%**
- **Combined practice increased GABA by 38%**

*"Meditation increases GABA, which reduces anxiety,"* stated researchers [9].

### The Power of Sangha (Community)

The word *sangha* means "community" in Buddhist tradition. The *sangha* is considered one of the "Three Jewels."

**Why sangha matters:**

1. **Mirroring**: The community reflects back to us our true nature
2. **Belonging**: We're not alone in our suffering
3. **Witness**: Someone sees us, understands us, accepts us
4. **Support**: Others help us when we're stuck

### Dharmic Community Healing Practices

2. **Satsang** (Hindu): Gathering to listen to Bhagavad Gita

4. **Metta Circle** (Buddhist): Sitting with others in meditation

### When to Seek Professional Help

**Seek professional help when:**

- You can't eat, sleep, or function in daily life
- You're thinking about harming yourself or others
- You've tried self-help strategies for 3-6 months without improvement
- Your symptoms are severe, chronic, or worsening

**Professional help is not opposite to Dharmic practice.** The best approach is **integrative**:

- Professional treatment for acute symptoms
- Dharmic practices for long-term healing
- Community support for ongoing recovery

## Section 11: A Warning and a Caution

### When Dharmic Practices May Not Work

Sometimes, Dharmic practices alone are not enough:

1. **Severe Mental Illness**: Bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, severe depression require professional treatment
2. **Trauma**: Severe childhood trauma may require specialized therapy
3. **Medical Conditions**: Physical illness can affect mental health
4. **Systemic Issues**: Social inequality, poverty, oppression require systemic change

### When Dharmic Practices Can Hurt

Dharmic practices can backfire if:

1. **Not Done Right**: Meditation can cause dissociation or psychosis in vulnerable people
2. **Used as Escape**: Practices used to avoid addressing life problems
3. **Used as Replacement**: Dharmic practices replace professional treatment when they shouldn't
4. **Spiritual Bypass**: Using spirituality to avoid addressing human pain
5. **Spiritual Abuse**: Teachers or communities using spirituality to control or exploit

### Guidelines for Safe Practice

1. **Start Small**: Begin with short, simple practices
2. **Be Consistent**: Regular practice is more important than long sessions
3. **Be Patient**: Healing takes time, be gentle with yourself
4. **Seek Support**: Community, family, friends, professionals
5. **Listen to Your Body**: If something feels wrong, stop
6. **Integrate, Don't Replace**: Combine Dharmic practices with professional treatment when needed

The Dharmic path to mental health is not about fixing broken minds, but awakening healthy minds. It's about:

- Understanding who we are
- Cultivating inner stillness
- Connecting with something larger
- Living ethically and compassionately
- Healing the body, mind, and spirit together

As the *Bhagavad Gita* (4.7-8) says:

> *"In every age, I appear in a form—be it a cow or a lion or a human being—as a savior to the distressed. Those who surrender to Me, I grant them all their desires."*

The path to mental health is a path of surrender, not struggle. A path of connection, not control. A path of wholeness, not just symptom management.

As we embrace the Dharmic path to mental health, may we find:

- **Peace** in our minds
- **Joy** in our hearts
- **Wisdom** in our understanding
- **Compassion** in our relationships
- **Wholeness** in our being

As the *Rig Veda* (10.124.1-2) declares:

> *"The Divine is One. The sages know it as truth. They call Him variously: God, Spirit, Immortal, Man."*

May we know this truth, and may our minds, hearts, and lives reflect it.
